Election Day is coming up, and I've been thinking about what it actually means to "get involved." This past year I tried it two very different ways: once from my desk, writing code for a civic-tech nonprofit, and once in person, holding a clipboard at a rally. Both taught me something, and they taught me different things.

## Getting involved remotely: WeVote

I got into volunteering almost by accident. I was contracting and job hunting when I came across a LinkedIn post for a volunteer software position. I'd always been drawn to open source, and it clicked: why not open source *volunteering*? So I applied, interviewed, and spent about six months working on their app while I kept up the job search.

[WeVote](https://wevote.us/) is a nonpartisan nonprofit trying to get people excited about civic engagement. It gives voters a place to see what's on their ballot and who's endorsing what, gives candidates a way to connect with the people they want to represent, and supports broader education and outreach between candidates and voters. It's a genuinely interesting product: a public place for candidates to show why they're fit for office, and for voters to share where they stand.

My own contributions were mostly small: bug fixes, layout tweaks, a few new pieces of UI. Honestly, the thing I'm proudest of isn't any one change. It's that I stood up and engaged at all. If my work was a drop in the bucket, well, what is an ocean but a multitude of drops?

It also turned out to be a great stepping stone for me. I hadn't done much front-end work before, and WeVote's stack uses tools that are common across the industry, which helped when I applied for new roles. It was also when I really got comfortable with AI tooling. Once I started using it to handle some of the tedious parts of front-end work (so many CSS rules), my productivity took off. A lot of what I took away from those six months was the learning and the tools I picked up along the way.

A few other lessons stuck with me:

- **Open source is a different world.** Coming from big tech, the difference in process was stark: much less red tape, and also fewer safety nets. It was interesting to see how priorities get set when everyone is a volunteer.
- **Remote, part-time engagement is hard.** With one meeting a week and no fixed hours, it was easy to drift. I've found I do better with more structure and clear expectations, which is easier to get in a full-time role.
- **Mission matters for motivation.** What kept me going was the mission: civic education and engagement. I was less sure about parts of the product, but I never doubted the values and motivation behind it.

Eventually I was hired onto another contract. With six months behind me, I felt good about what I'd learned. I'd also seen burnout up close earlier in my career, so I made the call to step back rather than spread myself too thin.

## Getting involved locally: Voter Choice Arizona

My second way in was completely spontaneous. At a "No Kings" rally in Phoenix, I walked past a table for [Voter Choice Arizona](https://voterchoicearizona.org/), a nonpartisan, volunteer-led group that educates voters about ranked choice voting (RCV) and open primaries. I picked up a clipboard and started canvassing right then and there.

The timing was lucky. The rally was in Phoenix, but the group was looking to grow its presence in Tucson, where I live. Tucson is something of a satellite hub, so events are less frequent, a couple of times a year. I've collected signatures and talked with people about RCV at places like a vegan night market and a community housing event. The group also has a speaker training program, so now and then I do some public speaking too.

What I like most about RCV is also the thing people most often misunderstand: **it isn't a partisan issue.** The idea is a system that makes politicians accountable to more of their voters and rewards working together, rather than the deadlock we keep getting. Right now the group is working on local efforts, like a petition drive in Tempe, while it builds toward a statewide measure.

The best part has been the people. I'll never forget that first day of just putting on a shirt and doing something that felt personally meaningful, and meeting a couple of the organizers who keep the group running. At the housing event, the person I shared a table with also volunteers for a classical music appreciation club, which I thought was so cool. Sitting next to someone for a few hours and hearing about their hobbies and passions is a completely different experience from a structured video call.

## Remote vs. local

Neither is better. They're just different:

- **Remote** let me use the skills I already had, on my own schedule, for a national effort. It also took real discipline to stay engaged.
- **Local** asked less of my technical skills and more of my willingness to talk to strangers. In return, it gave me a community.

If you've been meaning to get involved, pick whichever one fits your life right now. Either way, you're one more drop in the bucket.

## Before Election Day

If you're in Arizona, here's where things stand for the November 3 general election:

- **The registration deadline (October 5) has passed**, and Arizona doesn't have same-day registration. If you missed it this time, register now for the next election.
- **Request a mail ballot by October 23**, if you want one.
- **In-person early voting runs through October 30.**
- **Find your polling place and track your ballot** at [my.arizona.vote](https://my.arizona.vote/WhereToVote.aspx). Election Day polling places can differ from early voting sites, so check before you go.

Wherever you live, take some time to learn what's actually on your ballot. Read the information packet if you got one in the mail, research the candidates and where they stand, and look up the ballot questions. Tools like [WeVote](https://wevote.us/) can help you see your ballot in one place. And maybe, dare I say it, let yourself get excited about a candidate or a ballot question.

See you at the polls.
